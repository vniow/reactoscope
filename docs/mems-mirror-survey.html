MEMS mirror survey

Twelve Mirrorcle-adjacent repositories dropped into mems/ — vendor control code, firmware, and research projects — read against one question: what would a MEMS Laser Scanner Model need that the built Galvo Laser Scanner Model doesn't already give it?

Galvo Scanner Model (built) MEMS mirror (this survey)
Finding 1

Mirrorcle's own driver docs group mirrors into three drive regimes — quasistatic, resonant-quasistatic, and resonant — and only the quasistatic case tracks an arbitrary commanded trajectory the way a galvo does.

Finding 2

The vendor's own docs call the mirror "a high-Q spring-mass system" — confirming CONTEXT.md's framing verbatim. ζ → 0, the case the Galvo Scanner Model flags as unphysical, is the MEMS mirror's normal resonant operating point.

Finding 3

Driving a resonant mirror above its natural frequency can physically damage it. The one repo built around this problem is a notch filter, not a smarter controller — safety here means removing a frequency band, not sensing and backing off.

Finding 4

Real closed-loop position control barely exists across these twelve repos. The two firmware projects attempting it are unfinished; the most complete tracking algorithm (FoveaCam) has only run in simulation.

Finding 5

At least one real instrument here (BiScan) pairs a MEMS mirror on the fast axis with a galvo on the slow axis. A MEMS Laser emulator may need to coexist with the Galvo one, not replace it.

Hardware capabilities

Drive electronics

Differential high-voltage electrostatic drive: XIN/YIN each split across two DAC channels as Vbias ± Vdiff/2. Low output current, onboard DC/DC boost converter. Four embedded Bessel low-pass filters — cutoff set by an external clock (FCLK), independently per axis, per the mirror's own datasheet recommendation.

Interfaces

Analog Input driver variant: three voltages (X, Y, intensity) for a DAQ card, FPGA, or bench supply. USB driver variant (PlayzerX): binary serial protocol, a 125,000-sample FIFO (83,333 for RGB), sample rate 200–50,000 S/s, default 22,000.

Mirror modes

Vendor taxonomy: quasistatic, resonant-quasistatic, resonant. Only the quasistatic case behaves like a positioner tracking an arbitrary target; the other two are defined by their relationship to one natural frequency.

Calibration

Every mirror ships with a per-unit static-response report: voltage → mechanical angle, and it's nonlinear. CAnDLE in this set is exactly that measurement redone with a camera and a laser spot instead of the vendor's bench rig.

Limitations

Resonance is a hazard, not just a constraint

Operating a resonant mirror above its natural frequency risks damaging it. The dedicated response to this in the survey is a notch filter on the driver input (Filter-For-Mirrorcle-MEMS-Drivers), citing a two-photon imaging paper's split of scanner behavior into a safe region below the oscillator frequency and a hazardous one above it. The safety envelope here is enforced by removing a frequency band from the signal — there's no sense-and-back-off controller anywhere in this set.

Closed-loop control is aspirational, not proven

MEMS_mirror_close-loop_firmware's own commit log calls its phase-difference calculation unverified against its MATLAB reference. Everything else that moves a real mirror in this survey — the lens-control functions, LD_MemsMirror, the Gesture Recognition thesis — sends open-loop voltage commands to a mirror characterized in advance. FoveaCam's differentiable tracking algorithm is the most complete closed-loop idea here, but it has only run against a simulated mirror, never real hardware, in this repo.

Repo quality varies a lot

Several of these are one-off research or thesis archives, not maintained libraries: the STM32 structured-light repo explicitly disclaims being a general-purpose MEMS control library, and the Gesture Recognition thesis notes its hardware "is no longer accessible." playzerx is the one actively maintained, CI-tested project in the set — it's Mirrorcle's own current product SDK.

What this means for a MEMS Scanner Model

The Galvo Scanner Model's damped-biquad-plus-slew form assumes the thing it's modeling is trying to track wherever you point it. A resonant MEMS mirror isn't doing that — it's a near-undamped oscillator you excite at one frequency, and "position" falls out as the phase of that oscillation. A faithful MEMS Laser emulator likely needs two sub-forms behind the same seam rather than one: a quasistatic mode that can reuse the existing damped-servo shape almost as-is, and a resonant mode whose state is amplitude/frequency/phase, not a filtered copy of a commanded X/Y signal — plus a hard exclusion band around the mirror's natural frequency, since here that's a safety limit, not a tuning choice. Blanking may also need to key off the resonant sweep's own phase (as the structured-light firmware does) rather than free-run against X/Y the way the CRT and Galvo emulators do.
