Sample rate and buffer

The Controller holds a circular FIFO and reads it at a fixed rate; the host writes into it over USB. Those are two different rates, and the limits are quoted differently in five places — worth knowing before you pick a number.

The spread is not contradiction so much as layering: 50–50,000 is what the protocol will accept, 200–50,000 is what the C++ API documents, and the tighter ranges belong to particular generators that have their own reasons. For a simulator, 50–50,000 is the honest outer bound and 22,000 is the number the device actually boots with.

Repeatability sits at about one position step

<0.01° repeatability against a 0.0083° quantum means the device repeats to roughly one LSB. The 12-bit grid is therefore a real floor, not a number that disappears under mechanical noise — a simulator that models position as continuous is being optimistic by about one part in 4096 of full scale.

Speed: bandwidth, settling, and point-to-point

This is the section that answers "how far can I push it." The headline is that PlayzerX is specified at dc to ~2200 Hz on both axes, and that figure is not a mirror property — it is the bandwidth the hardware filter is deliberately set to.

All four drive the same mirror. The difference is entirely in how the command is shaped before it gets there — which is why the filter, not the mirror, is the thing a simulator most needs to model.

Point-to-point, as the API frames it

MTIDevice::GoToDevicePosition(x, y, m, mSec = 5) is the explicit point-to-point call. Its documentation is the clearest statement of the tradeoff anywhere in the material:

Note that the actual step time of the device from stopped position to new position is ultimately a function of both the stated (mSec) parameter and the bandwidth setting of the hardware filters … if the requested step time is too short for a given MEMS mirror design, and HardwareBandwidthBw is too high, the device may take a longer time to settle into new position while overshooting and ringing about the position. mems/playzerx-master/mtidevice/include/MTIDevice.h

Two things stand out. The default step time of 5 ms is ten times the filtered settling time — the API is conservative by an order of magnitude, which tells you how seriously the vendor treats ringing. And PointToPointPattern's floor of six samples is, at the default rate, shorter than the mirror can settle in: the generator will happily construct a pattern the mirror cannot actually follow, leaving it to the filter to prevent damage.

The damage boundary is explicit, not implied

Passing mSec = 0 to GoToDevicePosition steps the mirror as fast as the hardware filter permits. The header says plainly that this "can potentially damage or destroy the device if hardware filter bandwidth is not set to recommended or reasonably low limit."

That is the one place in all of this material where the vendor names destruction rather than degradation, and it is worth carrying into any simulator as the thing the resonance-margin readout exists to warn about. The mechanism is the same one the PlayzerX programming guide describes: the mirror is a high-Q spring-mass system, and a fast enough step excites it at resonance.

Settling depends entirely on the criterion

The single most important thing to understand before quoting any speed figure for this device: settling time varies twelvefold depending on what counts as settled, and Mirrorcle's published numbers do not all use the same criterion. Measured from reactoscope's model at its calibrated defaults:

Settled to withinTimeMoves/secSource
5% of full scale333 µs3000derived
2% of full scale542 µs1846derived this is what "~500 µs" means
1%1729 µs578derived
1 LSB (0.049%)6458 µs155derived the device's own resolution

A Q=25 resonance has an envelope time constant of 1447 µs, so crawling the last decade — from 1% down to one position step — costs 4.4 ms on its own. That is why the two figures diverge so violently.

One parameter set satisfies every published number

Filter corner 1800 Hz, mirror 5500 Hz at Q 25:

system bandwidth 2191 Hz against a spec of ~2200 · filtered settling 542 µs to 2% against ~500 µs · unfiltered settling 11.9 ms to 1 LSB against ~10 ms · settling to 1 LSB 6.5 ms against GoToDevicePosition's 5 ms default.

That last agreement is the satisfying one. The API's 5 ms default step time is not arbitrary caution — it is approximately how long this mirror needs to reach its own resolution.

The trap: system bandwidth is not the filter corner

PlayzerX's "dc to ~2200 Hz" is measured at the output. A high-Q resonance peaks the response and drags the system −3 dB point well above whatever the filter's own corner is. Setting a filter corner to 2200 Hz builds a device with a 2977 Hz system bandwidth that rings for four times as long.

This cost an entire debugging session, during which the evidence pointed convincingly at the mirror's Q being wrong. It was one default carrying the wrong units.

Input shaping: the real headroom, and its price

Everything above concerns the lowpass approach. A zero-vibration shaper — two impulses whose mirror responses cancel — does substantially better, and Mirrorcle's inverse-filter variant better still. Measured here against the 1 LSB criterion:

Technique2%1 LSBmoves/s
lowpass only500 µs6083 µs164
ZV shaper alone83 µs2604 µs384
ZV shaper + relaxed lowpass208 µs1521 µs658
ZV shaper mistuned by 5%1979 µs7688 µs130

Four times the moves per second — but only if you know the mirror's resonance to within a couple of percent. Mistune by 5% and most of the gain is gone; by 20% and it is worse than not shaping at all. That sensitivity is the entire commercial argument for closed-loop control, which Mirrorcle sells alongside and rates at a further ~4×.

An implementation constraint specific to audio-rate hosts

The shaper's second impulse lands half a damped period after the first — for a 5500 Hz mirror at 48 kHz, 4.36 samples. Round that to 4 and the cancellation fails badly enough that settling is worse than no shaper, because the residual must then decay through the mirror's own 1.4 ms envelope. Linear interpolation of the fractional delay fails the same way. Cubic Lagrange or an allpass is not a refinement here; it is the mechanism.

Still not modelled, deliberately

The hardware/software filter split. A full MTI controller has a settable HardwareFilterBw plus an optional software content filter; the model collapses them. On a real PlayzerX the hardware filter is fixed and unexposed, so you could not drive the mirror into resonance through content alone — the emulator lets you, which is right for an MTI controller and permissive for PlayzerX.

Voltage-to-angle nonlinearity. The MTIParam list has no linearisation entry, so the Controller does not correct it — this is an uncorrected real effect the model omits, not one the device handles. Omitted because inventing a response curve would fabricate exactly the per-unit characterisation data that should never be guessed.

Whether the Controller interpolates. MTIParam includes an undocumented InterpolationType with no enum and no comment. A zero-order hold is the conservative assumption; if the device interpolates, the model overstates stepping at low sample rates.
